                   Gene duplication provides a well-established route from promiscuous enzymes to specialised
                descendants, but specialisation can in principle also arise through selection acting on overlapping
                activities distributed across multiple proteins. We study a minimal origin–fixation model in which
                weak generalist proteins jointly supply a fixed set of organismal reactions and fitness depends on
                aggregate reaction capacity rather than on any single protein in isolation. Mutations act pleiotropically
                on loop-local sets of activities, are constrained by a tunable structural incompatibility term, and are
                evaluated in the context of the complete proteome. We systematically vary initial reaction redundancy,
                mutational asymmetry, and structural incompatibility. Across all 200 simulated conditions, proteins
                lost effective functional breadth and concentrated a larger fraction of their activity into their strongest
                function. Specialisation was substantially stronger under the symmetric mutation kernel than under
                the loss-biased kernel, showing that a bias toward biochemical loss was not required for the effect.
                Higher redundancy attenuated breadth loss most clearly under loss-biased mutation, while generally
                buffering the weakest organismal reaction against larger performance losses. Strong structural
                incompatibility did not monotonically increase specialisation and, at the largest value examined,
                generally reduced it. These results show that phenotype-level selection on overlapping catalytic
                functions can generate protein-level specialisation without gene duplication, while also revealing
                that the strength and form of that specialisation depend strongly on the mutational and structural
                constraints of the model.
